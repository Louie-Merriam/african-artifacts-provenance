# What the results indicate — interpretive analysis

*Computed from `out/FINAL_bm_africa_provenance_master.csv`, excluding the 1,108 Asia-department (Edo-homonym) records. African-corpus verdicts: 3,576 unfair · 1,465 transactional · 556 fair · ~4,600 insufficient · ~8,600 not shortlisted. Three caveats govern everything below: (1) this is the **British Museum's** collection, so it measures what ended up in British hands, not each empire's total extraction; (2) the corpus was harvested by episode keywords, so famous episodes are over-represented relative to a random sample of the BM; (3) verdicts are model-generated review signals with cited evidence, not determinations. Power/community assignments are derived from findspot/production place (mandate-aware for ex-German territories), so they are approximate.*

## 1. Which colonial powers did the unfair acquiring?

| ruling power of source territory | unfair | % of adjudicated | reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Britain | 1,893 | 34% | volume leader — unsurprising in Britain's own museum |
| Belgium (Congo Free State / Belgian Congo) | 901 | **57%** | the highest unfair *rate* of any major territory group |
| Ethiopia (never colonized; British 1868 expedition) | 218 | 41% | pure expedition loot — extraction without colonization |
| France | 27 | 12% | low **in the BM** — French loot went to Paris |
| Britain (ex-German mandate territories) | 15 | 13% | |
| Germany | 4 | 13% | low **in the BM** — German loot went to Berlin/Hamburg |

The France/Germany rows are the key interpretive lesson: the BM sample cannot rank empires. What it *can* show is structure: Dahomey material in the BM is only 12% unfair because the 1892 Abomey loot went to the Trocadéro, not London — the geography of empire is visible in the verdicts. Conversely, over half of everything judgeable from Belgian-ruled Congo in the BM is unfair — mostly material collected for the museum itself under Congo Free State-era conditions (Torday) or bought through the Brussels/Antwerp market.

## 2. Which regions were most looted (in this collection)?

| region | unfair | % of adjudicated | driver |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nigeria | 947 | 37% | Benin City 1897 dominates (Benin City alone: 678 of 1,055 mapped records unfair) |
| DR Congo | 900 | 57% | Kasai/Kuba collecting, Free State-era chains |
| Ghana | 476 | 36% | Kumasi 1874/1896/1900; Royal Palace findspots 96% unfair |

Volume and rate diverge: Nigeria has the most unfair objects; Zimbabwe and DR Congo have the highest *proportions* — collections whose judgeable records are majority-unfair.

## 3. Who got looted the most?

Source communities (place/episode-derived):

| community | unfair | % of adjudicated |
|---|---|---|
| Edo (Benin kingdom) | 845 | 62% |
| Congo-basin peoples (non-Kuba) | 560 | 51% |
| Asante | 507 | 49% |

At the level of individual courts, the answer is royal: **records with royal/palace findspots are 92% unfair (376 of 408)** — overwhelmingly the Asantehene's court (Manhyia Palace + Royal Palace Kumase ≈ 364 unfair), plus Cetshwayo's kraal (2/2) and the Oba of Benin's palace behind the Benin City numbers, and Tewodros II's Maqdala. The single most-looted parties in this collection are named sovereigns: the Oba of Benin, the Asantehene, Tewodros II, and the Kuba nyim.

The low Zulu rate is itself a finding: the BM's large KwaZulu-Natal holdings are mostly 20th-century photographic documentation and purchased beadwork (the Jolles collections), not Ulundi war loot — episode fame does not equal collection composition.

## 4. What contexts produced the FAIR verdicts?

Fair (n=556, African corpus) is overwhelmingly **modern and consensual**: median acquisition year **2006**; 314 of 556 acquired in the 2000s–2010s. Its contexts: purchases from named contemporary makers and market traders (David Heathcote's Hausa embroidery fieldwork purchases, 85; Donald Wilson, 39), works acquired from living artists (Ann Gollifer, 41), donated contemporary photography (Trust for African Rock Art, 24), and — the one striking historical cluster — **Thomas Bowdich's 1817 Asante mission gifts (26)**: diplomatic exchange with a sovereign Asante court, eight decades before the same court was sacked. The Asante appear at both poles of the dataset, which is the clearest internal demonstration that the system judges *circumstances*, not peoples.

Transactional (n=1,465; median year **1938**) is the colonial-era purchase economy: Fagg's documented field purchases (231), Wellcome Institute institutional transfers (177), Curle (158), Norman Hardy (117), Nadel (54), Macleod (37). Money demonstrably changed hands; the asymmetry of the colonial context is exactly the open historical question the category preserves rather than resolves.

## 5. The time signature of looting — and laundering

Unfair acquisitions by decade: peaks at the 1890s (626) and **1900s (1,219)** — the accessioning wave after Benin 1897 and the turn-of-century expeditions — with a median unfair year of **1908**. But unfair does not stop with empire: secondary waves in the **1940s (313)** and **1950s (421)** are old loot arriving late through dealer stock and estate transfers (Oldman's widow, Beasley's Cranmore, the Wellcome dispersal — whose decade also produces the largest insufficient-evidence pile, 1,321), and a 1990s bump (93) marks loot still surfacing a century after seizure. Fair verdicts are the mirror image, concentrated post-2000. In one sentence: **seizure happened in a generation; absorption took a century — and the late-arriving unfair verdicts are the laundering the pipeline was built to catch.**

## 6. Headline sentences (paper-ready)

- Of adjudicated records from Belgian-ruled Congo in the BM, 57% were judged unfair — the highest rate for any major territory; Kuba material alone reaches 72%.
- Records found at royal courts are 92% unfair: the most-looted parties in the collection are named sovereigns.
- Dahomey material in the BM is only 12% unfair because the 1892 loot went to Paris — the collection's verdicts trace the geography of empire.
- Fair acquisitions have a median year of 2006 and are dominated by purchases from living makers; unfair acquisitions have a median year of 1908 and recur in dealer-driven waves into the 1990s.

# Further analysis (round 2)

## 7. How much does the museum's own record admit? (explicit vs inferred unfair)

Only **741 of 3,576 unfair verdicts (21%) rest on explicit seizure language** in the museum's own record ("looted," "taken from," "punitive," "prize," "spoils," "captured"…). The other **79% required inference** from acquisition chains and context — flagged dealers, officer-collection lines, palace findspots, episode dates. Corpus-wide, explicit seizure language appears in just 1,184 records. This is the quantified ceiling of naive keyword search: it could reach at most ~1,184 candidates and ~741 of the unfair set, while the chain-based method reaches 3,576. (Of the explicit-language records, 332 still ended insufficient — largely depictions, secondhand mentions, and the 164 quote-validation downgrades.)

## 8. How the loot arrived: given, more than bought

Half of the unfair objects entered as **donations (1,783; 50%)** against 1,363 purchases (38%) and 102 bequests. The colonial establishment is visible in the donor lines: **22% of unfair records were delivered by a government body** (Gold Coast Government, Crown Agents, Secretary of State, Foreign Office), 9% by donors carrying military rank, 8% by institutions. The museum did not primarily buy its loot; it received it from the state and its officers.

## 9. The laundering tail, quantified (single-date episodes)

- **Benin 1897** (n=822 dated unfair): 44% accessioned within two years of the sack; **median lag 11 years; a quarter arrived more than 57 years after the looting; the latest in 1992 — 95 years on.**
- **Maqdala 1868** (n=88): 67% arrived immediately (median lag 0 — the 1868 wave via the Secretary of State), **but a quarter arrived 28+ years later and the last in 1990, 122 years after the assault.**

Seizure is an event; accession is a process spanning a century. This is the empirical justification for date-independent flagging.

## 10. What kinds of objects were looted (calibration, verified empirically)

Unfair share by object type (≥80 adjudicated) forms exactly the gradient the calibration rule predicts — court, ritual, and prestige objects at the top; trade goods, documentation, and currency at the bottom:

| type | unfair share | | type | unfair share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| plaque (Benin) | **90%** | | gold-weight (trade) | 24% |
| spear | 72% | | bowl | 23% |
| bead (royal, Kumasi) | 67% | | spoon | 18% |
| box | 67% | | smoking-pipe | 19% |
| sword | 59% | | cloth | 11% |
| ornament | 60% | | kuduo | 9% |
| necklace | 52% | | painting | 5% |
| figure | 40% | | coin | **3%** |

The 90%-vs-3% span between Benin plaques and coins is the calibration claim ("a court object via a flagged chain is strong; a trade good is weak") holding as a measured property of the output, not just a prompt instruction.

## 11. Archival silence: thin records escape judgment

Median provenance-text length by verdict: unfair 431 chars, transactional 462, fair 428 — but **insufficient_evidence records have a median of just 234 characters**, roughly half the documentation of any decided category. Verdictability tracks cataloguing: objects with thin records cannot be judged in either direction, which means the insufficient pile is not a clean bill of health but in part a measure of archival silence — and any future improvement in museum documentation would move records out of it in both directions.

## 12. Robustness check: is "unfair" over-assigned? (the Congo/Torday probe)

A fair challenge: only 21% of unfair verdicts carry explicit seizure language — could the other 79% be over-categorization? Probing the sharpest case (Torday's Kuba collection, famously *purchased* in the field):

- Of 811 Torday-linked unfair verdicts, **zero contain seizure language and ~94% mention purchase/collection** in their notes; the rationales rest on context ("collected... during the final years of the Congo Free State... under coercive conditions") — i.e., a **presumption standard**: colonial-era acquisition of royal/ritual material by a flagged expedition, absent documented consent, is treated as unfair. The model is not fabricating seizures; it is applying a burden-of-proof choice.
- It is not blanket condemnation: 156 Torday records came back transactional and 145 insufficient — records that specifically document the exchange get read as transactions.
- The purchase-language detector cannot cleanly separate field-side purchases (Torday ← Kuba) from museum-end purchases (BM ← Torday), so the middle band below is approximate.

**Sensitivity of the headline number to the evidentiary standard:**

| standard | unfair count |
|---|---|
| presumption standard (as run) | 3,576 |
| high-confidence only | 2,866 |
| excluding purchase-mentioned records with no seizure language | 2,212 |
| explicit seizure language in the record only | 741 |

Neither pole is right: explicit-only (741) is badly under-inclusive — museums euphemized, and the Manhyia armlet (no loot word, certainly loot) proves silence ≠ innocence; but the as-run figure bundles in ~1,400 expedition-purchase cases (heavily Congolese) where "unfair" encodes the Sarr-Savoy-style presumption that colonial-context acquisitions without proven consent are illegitimate, not evidence of seizure. **The honest presentation is the band, with the normative dial named**: 741 self-documented, ~1,471 context-inferred with silent records, ~1,364 purchase-mentioned-under-presumption. The Congo's 57% unfair rate sits mostly in the third band and should be discussed as presumption-driven; Benin City's 62% and Kumasi's 96% sit mostly in the first two.
