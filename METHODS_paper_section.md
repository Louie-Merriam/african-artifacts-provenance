## [Steps 2–6: as drafted]

The second step was to compile a set of red flags for the corpus. I defined a red flag as any documented element of an acquisition history that warrants closer review, organized into five types: episodes — specific extraction events with date windows, such as the British expedition against Benin City in 1897 or the German suppression of the Herero and Nama, 1904–1908; actors — named individuals documented as handling looted or coercively acquired material, including military officers, colonial officials, scientists, missionaries, dealers, and collectors; channels — the firms and institutions through which material moved, such as trading houses, auction rooms, mission societies, and colonial government offices; methods — the characteristic language of seizure that appears in museum records ("prize sale," "punitive expedition," "presented by" a campaign officer); and object priors — object types that are near-diagnostic of a particular event, such as a Benin brass plaque or an Ethiopian tabot. Alongside the red flags I encoded mitigating features (documented consent, an arm's-length modern sale, licensed excavation) so that the system could pull records back toward fair as well as push them toward unfair.

Red flags were compiled from documented sources only, under a fixed evidentiary standard: no name entered the list without a citable document linking it to unfairly acquired material, and every entry records that source together with a statement that its presence in a provenance is a signal for review, not proof of wrongdoing — the dealers on the list also sold legitimately acquired objects. I mined four classes of source. First, structured provenance registries: Digital Benin, whose full registry of 954 provenance names (784 persons, 167 institutions) I extracted through the site's content API; Open Restitution Africa, whose twenty-four published case studies I enumerated through the site's API; and the German Lost Art Foundation's Proveana database together with the PAESE project, whose colonial-context indexes tag persons and firms by occupation (military service, colonial administration, ethnographica trade, human remains) and colony. Second, museums' own provenance research, including the British Museum's contested-objects pages, the V&A's Maqdala and Asante research, the AfricaMuseum Tervuren's provenance dossiers, the Quai Branly's re-examination of the Mission Dakar-Djibouti, and National Museums Scotland's "Baggage and Belonging" catalogues of regimental collections, which attribute individual objects to named takers. Third, repatriation records, such as the Charité and University of Freiburg skull repatriations to Namibia and the return of the Witbooi Bible from the Linden-Museum. Fourth, the restitution scholarship, including the Sarr-Savoy report and studies of individual episodes. Names that could not be verified against a source were dropped and logged, and living figures were excluded unless named in published restitution findings. The final registry contained 221 actors and 90 channels spanning the major extraction episodes of British, German, French, and Belgian colonization and the twentieth-century "tribal art" market that laundered colonial loot into ordinary commerce. Because the detector matches literal strings, each name was expanded into alias variants — both name orders ("Webster, William Downing" and "William Downing Webster"), spellings with and without diacritics, and qualified forms only for common names — yielding, with the Digital Benin registry, roughly 2,900 matchable alias strings. Deliberately excluded from matching were entities so ubiquitous in the museum's records that they would flag everything (the Christy bequest, the major auction houses, the museum itself); these were retained only as context for the model. Finally, I fed the corpus back into the registry: frequency analysis of the acquisition-name fields (1,259 distinct names) revealed that the museum often records dealer stock under widow and estate names, so documented lines such as Dorothy Oldman (209 records) and the Wellcome Institute for the History of Medicine (1,272 records) were added as aliases, while frequent but undocumented names were set aside as candidates for future verification rather than flagged.

## [Step 3, elaborated: the flagging stage]

The third step was flagging: a deterministic pass over all 19,994 records that decides which acquisition histories deserve model scrutiny. Its design is governed by one failure mode above all others — over-flagging. A system that flags everything African would be useless as research and corrosive as rhetoric, so every choice in this stage trades a little recall for a lot of precision, and recall is then recovered through the breadth of the registry rather than through loose matching.

Flags are matched only against the acquisition record — the four acquisition-name fields, both acquisition-notes fields, and the curators' comments — and never against the object's physical description. This distinction matters more than it may appear: the corpus contains photographs of punitive expeditions, prints depicting battles, and commemorative medals naming campaigns, and matching descriptions would flag the depiction of looting as loot. Matching is exact, case-insensitive, diacritic-insensitive, and word-bounded: an alias fires only when it appears as a whole phrase. I used no fuzzy matching. Instead, recall lost to strictness is recovered by expanding each registry entry into its documented variant forms — both name orders, initialed forms, firm names, and the widow and estate lines under which museums actually record dealer stock. Common surnames are never aliases on their own ("W.D. Webster" can fire; bare "Webster" cannot, which keeps the unrelated donor Margaret Webster Plass safe), and names too common to qualify even in two-token form carry only fully qualified aliases. At this scale — roughly 2,900 aliases against twenty thousand records — per-pattern scanning is impractical, so matching runs as a single pass through an Aho-Corasick automaton with word-boundary checks applied at each candidate hit, which processes the corpus in seconds.

A record advances to adjudication only if it matches a named actor, a channel, a specific episode, or a seizure-method phrase. Matching a place or culture term alone is deliberately insufficient: on an all-African corpus, place terms would advance 89% of records (17,779 of 19,994, of which 7,321 matched nothing but a place), and the shortlist would cease to be a filter at all. Place matches are retained and passed to the model as context whenever a record advances on other grounds. Equally deliberately, the acquisition date is never a gate. Colonial loot was sold through the market for decades after each seizure, so an object that entered the museum in 1965 through a flagged dealer must still advance; date-in-window serves only as corroboration, never as a requirement. Under these rules, 10,458 of the 19,994 records advanced to the model.

Alongside the shortlist, and independent of it, I ran a weighted rule scorer over the full corpus as a second opinion. Where the shortlist is binary, the scorer stacks signals — an episode confirmed by a core place term plus at least one corroborator (a related place, an in-window date, loot language, or a palace or channel signal), explicit loot language, named actors, object-type-by-origin priors, colonial channels, royal or palace findspots, military-donor phrasing, and human-remains indicators — into a score with three bands (3,003 STRONG, 5,226 REVIEW, 11,765 low). Mitigating features (a modern acquisition year, depiction object types, numismatic material) cap circumstantial scores but never suppress named-actor, object-prior, loot-language, or human-remains hits, so a "clean" 1949 purchase of a Benin altar from a flagged dealer still scores STRONG. The scorer gates nothing; its purpose is triage order for human review and cross-validation of the model: agreement at the top of both distributions, and interpretable disagreement elsewhere, is evidence that neither method is merely echoing the other.

Throughout, a flag means precisely one thing: this record's acquisition history contains a documented element that warrants review. The shortlister is built to be over-inclusive within its precision constraints, because the next tier exists to disagree with it — the model receives every flag the record matched and is free to reject them, and in 43% of adjudicated cases it returned a verdict of insufficient evidence rather than endorsing the flags it was shown.

## [Steps 4–6: adjudication, validation, map — as previously drafted]

The fourth step was adjudication. Each shortlisted record was sent to Claude Haiku (claude-haiku-4-5) together with only the specific red-flag and mitigating entries it had matched. The model was instructed to judge solely from the record and the reference entries, and to return a structured verdict in one of four categories: unfair (concrete evidence in the record links the object to looting, seizure, or coercion), transactional (the record documents an ordinary purchase, sale, or exchange), fair (the record documents a clearly consensual acquisition, such as a commissioned or field-purchased work), or insufficient_evidence (the record is too thin to support any of these), together with a confidence level, verbatim evidence quotes, and a rationale. One instruction addressed laundering directly: a later clean-looking sale does not clear an object if a watch-listed dealer appears earlier in its chain, unless the record documents acquisition predating the relevant episode. Two guardrails constrained the model. Verdicts were forced through a structured output schema, and every evidence quote was validated as a verbatim substring of the record, with an unfair verdict that retains no verifiable quote automatically downgraded to insufficient_evidence. (Validation surfaced one failure mode — responses returning quotes split into single characters, which trivially passed the substring test — affecting 310 records; the validator was tightened to reject fragments under four characters and the 310 records re-adjudicated, demoting roughly 164 previously unsupported unfair calls.) The full run was executed through Anthropic's Message Batches API in approximately twenty-five minutes at a cost of about $17, with zero failed calls.

I validated the system in three ways. First, a laundered-case panel: twelve Benin objects that entered the museum decades after 1897 through dealers, with no loot language in their records, all of which a naive keyword search misses; the adjudicator returned unfair with high confidence on the well-documented chains and, appropriately, insufficient_evidence on the two weakest. Second, an accidental negative control: one harvest query ("Edo," intended for the Edo people of Benin) had also retrieved 1,108 Japanese Edo-period objects; the adjudicator returned zero unfair verdicts on them, indicating the system does not simply condemn whatever it is shown. Third, cross-validation against the independent deterministic scorer: the two systems agreed at the top of the distribution (57% of the scorer's strongest band was judged unfair), while their disagreements were interpretable — the model promoted 327 low-scoring records by supplying historical context the record omits, and returned insufficient_evidence on 827 strongly scored records where the rules stack circumstantial signals but the record itself offers nothing to quote.

Finally, I generated the map. All outputs were joined into a master table of the 19,994 records. For each record I parsed a location, preferring the find spot — where the object's journey to the museum began — over the production place, and geocoded it against a hand-built gazetteer of ~115 verified coordinates, including episode sites such as Maqdala, the Royal Palace at Kumasi, and Mushenge, the Kuba capital; no external geocoding service was used, so every coordinate is auditable. Records whose matched chain includes a documented intermediary hub are routed through it (Elder Dempster shipments through Liverpool, Trocadéro and Ratton material through Paris, Pareyn and Tervuren material through Brussels) before reaching London. Japanese Edo-period records, UK-origin records such as the museum's own photographs, and other non-African origins were excluded and are accounted for in the map's header. The result renders 15,367 records at 88 origin points as an interactive, self-contained HTML map, with origin markers sized by object count, colored by the share of unfair verdicts, and filterable by verdict category.
